Sam: The paper is about testing the sensitivity of the K-way energy probe on bidirectional predictive coding networks to the removal of cross-entropy. Specifically, it examines how the probe's performance changes when cross-entropy is replaced with mean squared error, or when using bidirectional predictive coding instead of standard predictive coding.
Alex: That's right. And to set the context, the K-way energy probe is a method used to analyze the representations learned by neural networks. Can you explain what the probe does and how it relates to the log-softmax margin?
Sam: The K-way energy probe is a way to measure the quality of the representations learned by a neural network. It's been shown that the probe's performance is closely related to the log-softmax margin, which is a measure of the difference between the predicted probabilities of the correct and incorrect classes. The probe essentially measures how well the network's representations can be used to distinguish between different classes.
Alex: Okay, got it. And what are the key assumptions underlying the reduction of the K-way energy probe to a monotone function of the log-softmax margin?
Sam: According to the paper, there are five assumptions, including the use of cross-entropy at the output and effectively feedforward inference dynamics. The authors want to test how sensitive this reduction is to the removal of cross-entropy, which is a key component of the standard predictive coding framework.
Alex: Exactly. So, what did the authors do in this study? What were the experimental conditions and how did they test the sensitivity of the probe to cross-entropy removal?
Sam: The authors used two conditions: standard predictive coding trained with mean squared error instead of cross-entropy, and bidirectional predictive coding. They tested these conditions on the CIFAR-10 dataset using a matched 2.1M-parameter backbone, and they ran the experiments across 10 seeds to ensure the results were robust.
Alex: And what were the main findings of the study?
Sam: The results showed that removing cross-entropy alone, without changing the inference dynamics, halves the probe-softmax gap. This suggests that cross-entropy is a major empirically load-bearing component of the decomposition. Additionally, the authors found that bidirectional predictive coding produces a smaller probe-softmax gap than standard predictive coding, but this effect is not due to greater latent movement in the bidirectional model.
Alex: That's interesting. Can you explain what you mean by "probe-softmax gap" and why it's significant?
Sam: The probe-softmax gap refers to the difference between the performance of the K-way energy probe and the softmax output of the network. A smaller gap indicates that the probe is more closely aligned with the softmax output, which is a desirable property. The fact that removing cross-entropy halves the gap suggests that cross-entropy is playing a crucial role in shaping the representations learned by the network.
Alex: Okay, I see. And what about the post-hoc temperature scaling ablation? What did that reveal about the probe-softmax gap?
Sam: The temperature scaling ablation showed that about 66% of the probe-softmax gap can be attributed to logit-scale effects, which can be removed by temperature rescaling. The remaining 34% reflects a scale-invariant ranking advantage of cross-entropy-trained representations. This suggests that cross-entropy training produces representations that are not only better calibrated but also more informative, even after accounting for differences in scale.
